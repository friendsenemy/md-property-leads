The one idea behind everything

Most people who sell a house for cash aren't in a hurry because they want to be. Something happened. Someone died and the house is stuck in their name. Taxes went unpaid and the county sold the debt to an investor. A court case ended and money is sitting unclaimed. Every tab on the dashboard finds one of those situations from public records, so we can reach the owner (or the heirs) with a real solution while every other investor is still buying the same tired mailing lists.

Where the data comes from

  • Obituaries posted in Maryland, scraped every morning.
  • SDAT — the state's property database. Every parcel in Maryland (2.4 million), who owns it, what it's worth, when it last changed hands. Rebuilt every Monday.
  • County tax-sale results — the list of properties whose unpaid taxes were sold to investors, with the bid amounts. Pulled monthly, and back through 2021 for the counties that keep old lists up.
  • Register of Wills estate records, entered by hand when we look one up.

What "free" means here

Every source is a public record. Nothing is bought, nothing is scraped from a site that forbids it. That matters when you're talking to a homeowner: we found them the same way a diligent title clerk would, not by buying their name.

Tab 1

Recent Death Leads

Recent Death Leads

What it finds

Someone in Maryland died this week, and they owned real estate. The tool matches the obituary to the state property database and shows you the house, what it's worth, and the owner's name on title.

Why they'll sell

The family now owns a house none of them lives in. It needs cleaning out, it has a mortgage or taxes due, and often three siblings in three states have to agree. A cash offer with a flexible closing date solves all of that at once.

A typical case

Widow dies in Waldorf, house in her name only

The obituary names two adult children in Virginia and Florida. The house is owner-occupied, no transfer in 30 years, assessed at $310,000. Neither child wants to move back. The lead shows up the morning the obituary posts.

What to say"I'm sorry for your loss. I buy houses in Charles County, and I know settling a parent's home from out of state is a lot. If it would help, I can make an offer that lets you close whenever you're ready and leave anything behind you don't want to deal with."

Timing rule: wait a respectful interval, and lead with help, not price. The families who call back are the ones who felt you weren't rushing them.

Tab 2

Title Leads

Title Leads · statewide

What it finds

Properties where the name on title belongs to someone who is already dead, sometimes for decades. Estates that never finished. "Heirs of" on the deed. A dead co-owner. The tool checks every parcel in Maryland against death records and estate files and gives each one a class and a priority score.

Why they'll sell

Nobody can sell, refinance, or insure a house titled to a dead person without fixing the title first. The heirs are often stuck and don't know where to start. Meanwhile taxes pile up. We can buy the property and handle the estate work as part of the deal.

Reading the class

D6
Owner died, then an heir died. Title never moved through either estate. The best kind.
D3
Estate is closed but the house is still in the dead person's name. Someone forgot the deed.
D1 / D5
Owner (or every owner) appears deceased in state death records.
E1 / E2
"Estate of" or "Heirs of" written right on the deed.
A real case · Charles County · class D6 · priority 91

Three parcels, 128 acres, titled to a man who died in 1997

Thomas Curry died in 1997 without a will. His estate opened and closed, but the land was never deeded out. His son Maynard, a co-owner on one parcel, died in 2008. In May 2026 the county sold the tax liens on all three parcels to three different investors. Total owed: $7,912. Assessed value: $835,000.

The dashboard shows both estates, the personal representative's name and address, the attorney who handled it, and a countdown until the investors can foreclose.

What to say"I'm reaching out about the family land on Thomas Curry's name. The tax liens on it were sold this spring, and after six months the buyers can start taking it. I'd like to make an offer that pays those off and puts real money in the family's hands before that happens."

A real case · Anne Arundel · Buy Window

Sold at tax sale two years in a row, nobody took it

A house assessed at $335,000 with about $2,000 in unpaid taxes was sold at the county tax sale in 2023, and again in 2024. Two different investors bid over $225,000 each time. Neither foreclosed. The owner still holds title and is still behind.

That's a person with a recurring problem and a house worth real money. Every other investor's list says "sold at tax sale" and moves on.

What to say"Your property's been on the county's tax sale list two years running. I can buy it, pay the county off at closing, and you walk away with the equity instead of watching it go to an investor for the taxes."
A real case · Prince George's · Lien Stranded + repeat

An investor bid $290K on a $104K house. Twice, nobody took it.

3801 Silver Park Dr, Suitland. Assessed at $104,000. At the 2026 sale an investor bid $290,154, nearly three times what it's worth. To take the deed they'd have to hand the county that $290K. They won't. The property is also tagged repeat: it was on the tax-sale list the year before too.

So the owners keep the house by default, the lien buyer is stuck, the owners have shown twice they can't get out on their own, and no other investor's list will ever surface it. That's the best combination on the tab.

What to say"Your house has been sold at the county tax sale two years running. The people who bought the lien can't realistically take it, but the debt doesn't go away either. I can buy it, clear the county at closing, and you'd walk away with real money instead of this coming back every spring."
A real case · Anne Arundel · Deed Conveyed (if it goes through)

A $1.26M house sold for $707K, over $8K in taxes

At the June 2026 sale, an investor bid $706,722 on a Riva property assessed at $1,258,633. Taxes owed: $8,002. If the investor forecloses and takes the deed, they pay the full bid to the county, and the county owes the former owner the difference: about $698,000.

Maryland law now requires the county to notify the former owner within 90 days and to have a simple claim process. Many never claim.

What to say"I'm not calling about buying anything. When your property went through tax sale, the buyer paid a lot more than the taxes. Under Maryland law that difference is yours to claim from the county, and I can point you to the form."

Surplus: the one rule that keeps us out of trouble

We tell people the money exists and where the free claim form is. We do not buy their claim, take an assignment of it, or charge a finder's fee. Maryland regulates that as a "foreclosure surplus acquisition" with contract rules and rescission rights, and a separate law voids finder's-fee agreements on money that's already gone to the state. Until a Maryland lawyer signs off, the surplus play is goodwill and relationship, and the same phone call is very often a buy conversation about something else they own.

Tab 4

Failed Foreclosures

Failed Foreclosures · §14-847

What it finds

Properties where a tax-sale investor won a court judgment and then didn't pay for it. When that happens, the court can hand the property to the county outright. The tool finds parcels the tax collector deeded to a county or town.

Why it's a deal

This isn't a homeowner lead. Nobody's losing a house; the county already owns it. It's an acquisition lead: counties don't want to hold houses. They sell them off by sealed bid, public sale, or direct negotiation, and a parcel that's been sitting on the county's books for a few years is one they'd like gone.

A real case · Cecil County · vested 2022

Two lots on E High St, Elkton, now owned by the town

213A and 213B E High Street. The tax collector deeded both to the Town of Elkton in 2022 after the foreclosure fell through. Assessed at $25,200 each. They've sat on the town's books since.

What to say (to the town)"I'm a local buyer. I see the town took title to two lots on East High in 2022. How does the town dispose of tax-sale property it's holding, and are those two available?"
Daily routine

How to actually work the dashboard

  1. Open the tab you're working. Each one loads on its own. The three numbers at the top tell you what's new.
  2. Sort by what matters. Title Leads: priority score. Surplus: estimated surplus or the overbid multiple. Click any column header. On the Surplus tab, tick "Repeats only" first: those are the owners who've failed to pay two years running.
  3. Click a row. The details window shows everything we know: the property, the owner, the money, the dates, the estate files, an aerial photo, and a "Next Step" written for that specific stage.
  4. Verify before you call. Every lead is built from records that can lag. For tax-sale leads, check the county's tax account page to confirm the lien is still open. For death leads, confirm the obituary matches the owner. Ten minutes of checking beats an awkward call.
  5. Mark it. Set the status to Contacted or Closed and leave a note. Statuses are saved in your own browser, so each of us keeps our own list. Export CSV pulls the whole filtered view into a spreadsheet for mailing.
  6. Come back Tuesday. The property scan reruns every Monday morning. Obituaries update daily. Tax-sale lists refresh on the 2nd of the month. One thing to expect: the Buy Window only holds sales that are at least six months old, so this year's May and June sales move in during November and December, county by county, on their own.
Know these cold

Six Maryland rules that decide the deal

1. A tax sale sells the debt, not the house.

The investor buys the lien. The owner still owns the property and can pay it off ("redeem") at any time until a court ends that right.

2. Six months before anyone can foreclose.

The investor can't even file until six months after the sale. That window is when the owner can still sell to us cleanly.

3. Two years or it dies.

If the investor never files within two years of the sale, their certificate is void and the money they paid goes toward the property's taxes. The owner keeps the house.

4. Redeeming is cheap before a lawsuit, expensive after.

Before the investor files, their attorney fee is capped at $500. After, it jumps to $1,300–$1,500 plus costs. Move early.

5. Surplus belongs to the former owner.

When an investor takes the deed, the bid minus the taxes is owed to whoever lost the property. The county must notify them within 90 days and can't require a court order to pay it.

6. Once there's a judgment, the owner has nothing to sell.

A final judgment ends the right of redemption. You can't buy from someone who no longer owns it. The opportunity lives before judgment, not after.

Words you'll see

Glossary

SDAT
Maryland's Department of Assessments and Taxation. The state property database.
Account number
The state's ID for a parcel. County code, then district, then account. Paste it into SDAT's site to see the record.
Lien / certificate
What the investor buys at a tax sale: the right to be repaid with interest, and to foreclose if they aren't.
Redeem
The owner pays off the lien plus interest and costs. Ends the investor's claim.
Foreclose the right of redemption
The court case the investor files to end the owner's right to pay it off and take the deed.
Struck
Nobody bid at the sale, so the county holds the lien itself.
Assessed value
The state's estimate of what the property is worth for tax purposes. Usually a bit under market.
Personal representative
The person the court put in charge of a dead person's estate. Often the one to call.
Register of Wills
The county office that holds estate files. Public, searchable by name.
Liber / Folio
Book and page number of a deed in the county land records. Look it up at mdlandrec.net to read the deed itself.
Repeat
The same parcel on the tax-sale list in more than one year. The owner didn't pay and nobody foreclosed, each time.
Surplus
What's left over after a tax-sale deed: the full bid minus the taxes, interest, and costs. Owed to the former owner.
